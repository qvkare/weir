Direction

Ex­change.

The feelingThe hammer falls, and the price is fair.

This is a market for a new commodity, the hour of an accelerator, and a room with two kinds of people in it: AI teams and their agents who need GPU time, and the people who own the GPUs. Markets have a century of physical culture we can borrow: the departure board, the sealed envelope, the paddle, the catalogue plate and the moment one price is called.

We set the brand in the language of that floor: a black board that flips, newsprint for the record, heavy condensed type for the call, and exactly three loud colours with fixed jobs, cobalt for buyers, saffron for sellers and vermilion for the price that belongs to neither.

It should read as an institution that has always existed and opened this week: grave enough to trust with next week's compute bill, alive enough that you want to watch the board.

Heatsink is the part of a GPU everyone recognises, and its job is to take the heat out. The product does that to a compute bill: it fixes next week's price for a GPU-hour, so the spikes never reach you. Working line: Takes the spikes out of the GPU bill. We keep heat literal and mechanical (fins, metal, the ticket), never flames, thermometers or red-to-blue.

Construction. A 56-unit tile with 6-unit corners and two 3-unit hinge pins on the half-height line. Letters are cut out of the tile on an 11 to 53 cap band, and a 1.8-unit seam at exactly half height cuts the letter but not the tile, as on a real flap (an early version cut the tile too and a W read as the won sign). Verticals 7.5, horizontals 6, thinner as in a real face. Every crossbar (H, E, A) sits wholly above the seam, so the flap never cuts a bar in half; that rule gives the face its high-waisted, departure-board look. The compact lockup sets the word in Big Shoulders Display 900 beside one flap; the eight-flap board is for signage and the hero.

Construction. A 60 by 34 ticket with 3.5-unit corners; two 4.5-unit bites mark the tear line a third of the way in, perforated by seven 0.9-unit holes. The stub carries one punch hole (the fill). Why a wall, not a diagram

The first screen has one job: make a judge feel this is a real market before reading a word. A board is the most recognisable image of a market there is, and it is generative here: every cycle deals a new round of lots, the ripples start from a random place, and the row order of the lock varies, so it never quite repeats.

Why not a wafer map or a rack wall? Considered and set aside: they say "hardware company", not "market", and a die map has no people in it. The board carries all three at once: the chip in its title row (H100 SXM, one GPU-hour, delivery week), the people as named bidders coloured by side, and the one called price down the middle. The hardware gets its own close-up below, drawn like a catalogue plate.

  1. Shuffle. Flaps ripple outward from a random point, about 3 s.
  2. Lock. Rows land top to bottom, each flap tumbling through two to four characters first, about 2.5 s.
  3. Hold. Every row shows a different lot and one vermilion column of the same price: one price for everyone. Still for 4.5 s.
  4. Release. A wave from the price column clears the board and the next round deals.

Canvas, no library, one cached glyph atlas, and only flipping cells are redrawn. It pauses off screen and in a hidden tab. Under reduced motion it draws the locked board once and stops. At 375 px the copy drops to the lower half over a scrim and the board compacts to side, size and price.

Who is in the room

Two kinds of people, one price

▲ Buyers hold LONG

AI teams, and the agents they run, who need GPU time next week. If the week's average price rises, LONG pays them the difference, so their compute bill holds.

An agent bids with a key that can commit and reveal but can never withdraw.
